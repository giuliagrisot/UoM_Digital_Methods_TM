What This Course Is About

Using R to analyse collections of texts while staying grounded in humanities interpretation.

  • Text analysis with R = practical methods for working with collections of texts.
  • The course keeps two things together: computational procedure and humanities interpretation.
  • Students will work with prepared examples and learn how to adapt the workflow to their own texts.
  • Transition: the course begins from what computational text analysis can make possible.

The Central Promise

Computational text analysis helps us notice patterns across texts that we can then investigate through interpretation and close reading.

  • Main claim: computation helps us notice patterns that are difficult to see text by text.
  • Patterns may include repetition, absence, contrast, association, change over time, or unusual uses of language.
  • These patterns do not replace interpretation; they direct attention.
  • Logical step: many texts become patterns; patterns become questions; questions send us back to reading.
  • Transition: because these outputs direct attention, we need to understand how they are produced.

The Central Caution

Computational results are shaped by corpus design, preprocessing, tools, and assumptions.

They are not neutral evidence.

  • Main claim: computational outputs are constructed evidence.
  • Results depend on corpus selection, metadata, preprocessing, tools, and model assumptions.
  • A table, graph, or score is therefore not neutral; it is the outcome of a workflow.
  • Transition: the workflow should begin with a humanities research question.

A Humanities Question

How is a theme represented across a collection of texts?

Examples:

  • space
  • emotion
  • gender
  • power
  • crisis
  • identity
  • Starting point: a theme or problem that matters for interpretation.
  • A theme is not necessarily one keyword; it can involve clusters of words, places, metaphors, relations, or affective tones.
  • Course examples will include space, emotion, and semantic neighbourhoods.
  • Transition: to study a question computationally, it has to become a workflow.

The Workflow

  • Research question: what needs to be understood?
  • Corpus: which texts can support that question?
  • Metadata: what information describes the texts?
  • Preprocessing: how do texts become analysable data?
  • Exploration: what visible patterns appear first?
  • Modelling: what further relations can methods reveal?
  • Interpretation: what can be claimed, and with what limits?
  • Transition: the corpus is the first major methodological decision.

What Is a Corpus?

A corpus is a body of naturally occurring language, assembled for a purpose.

It is usually:

  • machine-readable
  • made of authentic texts
  • sampled deliberately
  • representative of a language, genre, period, or text type
  • Corpus = body of texts or language data.
  • Key point from corpus linguistics: a corpus is generally assembled with particular purposes in mind.
  • Machine-readable: the texts can be processed by software.
  • Authentic: the texts are real uses of language, not invented examples.
  • Sampled: the corpus is selected rather than accidental.
  • Representative: not representative of everything, but of a defined language, genre, period, community, archive, or text type.
  • Transition: representativeness becomes clearer when contrasted with things that are not corpora.

What Is Not a Corpus?

Not every text collection is automatically a corpus.

Collection Why It May Not Be a Corpus
a list of words no textual context
a random folder of files no sampling logic
a set of quotations selected because they contain something
the whole web unstable, undefined, constantly changing
  • A list of words is vocabulary, not a corpus.
  • A random archive or folder may become a corpus, but only after selection criteria are defined.
  • A set of quotations is already filtered around what someone wanted to cite.
  • The web is not a stable corpus because its size and contents are undefined and constantly changing.
  • Logical step: corpus construction means turning available texts into a defined object of analysis.
  • Transition: because the corpus is designed, its design choices affect the results.

Corpus Choices Shape Results

Important decisions include:

Decision Why It Matters
Inclusion / exclusion Defines the evidence base
Genre and period Shapes what can be compared
Metadata Enables grouping and filtering
OCR / digitisation Affects what the computer can read
Missing texts Produces absences in the analysis
  • Inclusion/exclusion determines the evidence base.
  • Genre, period, language, translation, and edition shape comparability.
  • Metadata determines what can be grouped, filtered, and compared.
  • OCR and digitisation quality affect what the computer can read.
  • Missing texts and archival bias can be amplified by computational methods.
  • Transition: after corpus design, the first analyses are often simple but powerful.

First Methods: Counting and Context

Simple methods can have strong interpretive value:

  • frequencies
  • keywords
  • concordances / KWIC
  • collocations
  • Frequencies: what appears often?
  • Keywords: what appears unusually often in one group compared with another?
  • KWIC/concordance: where does a word appear, and in what immediate context?
  • Collocations: which words tend to appear together?
  • These methods connect pattern detection with contextual reading.
  • Logical step: begin with description before moving to interpretation or modelling.
  • Transition: concordances show the first move from counting to context.

Example: Concordance

A concordance lists occurrences of a word or phrase across the corpus.

Text Concordance Line
Novel A she arrived in the city after dark
Novel B the city seemed louder than before
Novel C no one in the city knew his name
Novel D beyond the city, the road opened
  • Concordance = a list of occurrences across texts.
  • It answers: where does this term appear, and how often does it appear in different places?
  • It can reveal variation across texts, authors, genres, or periods.
  • Logical step: concordance moves from counting a word to locating that word in the corpus.
  • Transition: KWIC narrows the concordance view to the immediate left and right context.

Example: KWIC

Keyword in context for city:

Left Context Keyword Right Context
the noise of the city followed her into the room
beyond the lamps of the city the fields began to darken
he returned to the city with no money and no plan
  • KWIC keeps the keyword attached to its immediate textual context.
  • It supports movement between pattern and example.
  • Repeated contexts can suggest themes: movement, contrast, class, emotion, place.
  • Logical step: after finding patterns, inspect actual language before making claims.
  • Transition: collocations summarise repeated word associations across many contexts.

Example: Collocations

Collocations identify words that tend to appear near each other.

Target Word Frequent Neighbour Possible Interpretive Prompt
city street movement, public space, circulation
city noise sensory experience, modernity, crowding
city poor class, inequality, social observation
city lights night, spectacle, urban atmosphere
  • Collocation = repeated co-occurrence, not just one example.
  • It answers: which words are associated with the target more often than expected or more often than we notice through reading alone?
  • Collocations can suggest themes, discourses, metaphors, or social categories.
  • They still need interpretation: association is not explanation.
  • Transition: sentiment analysis also begins with words, but assigns them affective values.

Sentiment Analysis

Sentiment analysis can help explore affective patterns across texts.

But it is limited by:

  • lexicons
  • genre
  • irony
  • historical language
  • negation
  • narrative voice

wonderful -> positive
sadness -> negative
not happy -> depends on negation handling

  • Sentiment analysis uses rules, lexicons, or models to assign affective values.
  • It can show broad patterns across texts, documents, or narrative time.
  • It is not the same as meaning, emotion, or reader response.
  • Limits include irony, genre, historical language, negation, and narrative voice.
  • Example logic: “wonderful” is easy for a lexicon; “not happy” requires handling negation; irony and narrative voice are harder.
  • Transition: word vectors offer a different way to study meaning through context.

Word Vectors

Word vectors help us explore semantic neighbourhoods.

They ask: which words are used in similar contexts?

  • Word vectors represent words numerically according to their contexts.
  • Words used in similar contexts are placed closer together in the model.
  • This makes it possible to explore semantic neighbourhoods.
  • Humanities use: ask what associations a corpus produces around a theme.
  • Transition: a spatial theme gives a concrete example.

Example: Analysing Space

Compare thematic neighbourhoods around terms such as:

Urban Seeds Rural Seeds
city country
town field
street garden

Possible questions:

  • What words appear near each theme?
  • Do the associations differ by author, genre, or period?
  • Which examples need close reading?
  • Start with seed words for a theme: city, town, street, country, field, garden.
  • Train or use a word-vector model on the corpus.
  • Inspect nearest neighbours around those seed words.
  • Compare whether different spatial terms produce different associations.
  • Return to KWIC and close reading to interpret the results.
  • Transition: this return to reading applies to every method in the course.

From Output Back to Reading

Tables, scores, and plots are not conclusions.

They are prompts for returning to examples, context, and argument.

  • Output is an intermediate object, not a conclusion.
  • Ask where a pattern comes from: which texts, authors, periods, sentences, or genres?
  • Check whether the pattern survives contextual reading.
  • Build claims by connecting computational pattern, textual evidence, and methodological limits.
  • Transition: the hands-on work follows this full cycle.

What You Will Do Hands-On

In this course you will:

  • analyse frequencies, KWIC, and collocations
  • run sentiment analysis

  • adapt scripts to your own material

  • Transition: applying the workflow requires thinking about the texts students bring.

Working With Your Own Texts

Before analysing your own material, ask:

  • What texts do I have?
  • What metadata do I need?
  • What question can this corpus answer?
  • What are its limits?
  • Identify the text collection: novels, interviews, newspapers, documents, letters, reviews, web texts.
  • Identify the metadata needed: date, author, genre, source, location, category.
  • Match the question to the corpus: what can this collection support?
  • Identify constraints: format, copyright, cleaning, OCR, language, size, missing texts.
  • Transition: the opening prompt turns this into a first research design exercise.

Write down:

  1. one text collection you might analyse
  2. one possible research question

  • Step 1: name a possible text collection.
  • Step 2: formulate one question or theme.
